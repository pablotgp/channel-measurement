\section{Different measurement instruments: the invisible bias}\label{sec:instruments}

A variable missing in one channel is the most visible case of a general problem: each channel measures with its own instrument. Keeping the notation of Section~\ref{sec:channel}, let $X^*$ be the latent covariate, taking values in a standard Borel space $\X^*$, with laws $P^*$ and $Q^*$ in the target group and the donor channel. Each channel records $X=\varphi_D(X^*)$, with $\varphi_0,\varphi_1\colon\X^*\to\X$ measurable, so that $P=P^*\circ\varphi_1^{-1}$ and $Q=Q^*\circ\varphi_0^{-1}$. Let $\mu^*(x^*)=\E[Y(0)\mid X^*=x^*,D=0]$.

\begin{theorem}[Different measurement instruments]\label{thm:measurement}
Assume (A1), latent support $P^*\ll Q^*$ and latent (A2): $\E[Y(0)\mid X^*,S=1]=\mu^*(X^*)$ $P^*$-almost surely.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Common variables.} For every measurable map $\xi$ on $\X^*$, $P^*\circ\xi^{-1}\ll Q^*\circ\xi^{-1}$. In particular, if a measurable $\upsilon$ on $\X$ satisfies $\upsilon\circ\varphi_0=\upsilon\circ\varphi_1$ (variables both channels measure the same way), the laws of $W=\upsilon(X)$ in the two groups satisfy $P_W\ll Q_W$.
  \item \emph{Identification with the common variables.} If, in addition, $\mu^*(X^*)=\E[Y(0)\mid W,D=0]$ $Q^*$-almost surely, Theorems~\ref{thm:identification}--\ref{thm:lebesgue} apply with $W$ as covariates.
  \item \emph{Differential measurement with support.} If $\varphi_0$ is injective and $P\ll Q$ (on standard Borel spaces, $\varphi_0$ then has a Borel image and a measurable inverse by the Lusin--Souslin theorem \citep[Theorem 15.1]{kechris1995}, and $\varphi_1(X^*)$ lies in that image $P^*$-almost surely because $Q$ is concentrated on it), the weighting estimator with $X$ converges, for bounded $Y$, to
  \[
  \theta_X=\int_{\X^*}\mu^*\bigl(\varphi_0^{-1}(\varphi_1(x^*))\bigr)\,P^*(dx^*),\qquad
  \theta_X-\theta=\int_{\X^*}\bigl[\mu^*\circ\varphi_0^{-1}\circ\varphi_1-\mu^*\bigr]\dd P^*.
  \]
  \item \emph{Indistinguishability.} Under the conditions of 3, there is a world without measurement error, with $\varphi_1=\varphi_0$ and the latent law of the target group equal to $P^*\circ(\varphi_0^{-1}\circ\varphi_1)^{-1}$, that reproduces the law of the target-group and donor-channel data (and of all observed data if, in addition, the recorded covariates of every premium unit have a law absolutely continuous with respect to $Q$), satisfies the maintained assumptions and has $\theta=\theta_X$. No procedure based only on the observed data distinguishes $\theta$ from $\theta_X$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(1) If $Q^*(\xi^{-1}(B))=0$, latent support gives $P^*(\xi^{-1}(B))=0$. If $\upsilon\circ\varphi_0=\upsilon\circ\varphi_1$, then $W=\upsilon(\varphi_D(X^*))=(\upsilon\circ\varphi_0)(X^*)$ in both channels, and it suffices to apply the above with $\xi=\upsilon\circ\varphi_0$.

(2) This is Proposition~\ref{prop:drop} with $V=W$, a function of $X^*$.

(3) In the donor channel, $X=\varphi_0(X^*)$ and $\varphi_0$ is injective with a measurable inverse, so conditioning on $X$ is equivalent to conditioning on $X^*$; by (A1), $m_0(x)=\mu^*(\varphi_0^{-1}(x))$ $Q$-almost surely, and since $P\ll Q$ this version is fixed $P$-almost surely. The weighting estimator converges to $\int m_0\dd P$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:identification}, which uses only $P\ll Q$ for this convergence), and by the change of variables for image measures, $\int m_0\dd P=\int m_0(\varphi_1(x^*))\,P^*(dx^*)=\theta_X$. By latent (A2) and the tower property, $\theta=\int\mu^*\dd P^*$.

(4) In the alternative world the donor channel does not change. The target group is recorded with $\varphi_0$ and its latent law is $\tilde P^*=P^*\circ(\varphi_0^{-1}\circ\varphi_1)^{-1}$, so its observed law is $P^*\circ(\varphi_0\circ\varphi_0^{-1}\circ\varphi_1)^{-1}=P^*\circ\varphi_1^{-1}=P$, the same as in the real world. For its observed outcome it suffices to take, given $X$, the same conditional law as in the real world, and under latent (A2) its response in the basic channel is $\mu^*$; its $\theta$ is $\int\mu^*\dd\tilde P^*=\theta_X$. The alternative world satisfies the assumptions: since $\varphi_0$ is injective, $Q^*(A)=Q(\varphi_0(A))$ and $\tilde P^*(A)=P(\varphi_0(A))$, so $P\ll Q$ gives $\tilde P^*\ll Q^*$, and latent (A2) holds by construction. Under the additional condition, premium units outside the target group are treated in the same way, transforming their latent law by $\varphi_0^{-1}\circ\varphi_1$, which is then defined almost surely; without it, $\varphi_1(X^*)$ may fall outside the image of $\varphi_0$ for those units, and a world with $\varphi_1=\varphi_0$ cannot reproduce them.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Reading.} A variable measured differently is more dangerous than a missing one. The missing one breaks support and usually leaves a trace: the ideal classifier is certain, $p=1$, and $\hat\varepsilon>0$ (the exception, a ceiling in one source, is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lalonde}). The differently measured one can preserve support, and the classifier interprets the measurement difference as a real difference between units. In an example with $P^*=N(1,1)$, $Q^*=N(0,1)$, $\mu^*(x)=\sigma(-3-0.8x)$, with $\sigma(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})$, and a premium channel that records the variable shifted by $\Delta$, a shift of $\Delta=\pm0.3$ standard deviations produces biases of $-20.5\,\%$ and $+25.4\,\%$ on $\theta=2.92\,\%$. In a small simulation of this example (details in the repository), the weighting estimator with a logistic classifier matches $\theta_X$ ($z=+0.6$ in both cases). The AUC changes (0.76 without error; 0.82 and 0.69 with it), but in a way that a real population difference would also produce. The only way out is information external to the design: units measured with both instruments, for instance customers who switched channels, which allow estimating $\varphi_0^{-1}\circ\varphi_1$.

\subsection{Evidence from public data: self-reported versus measured body mass index}\label{sec:nhanes}

Theorem~\ref{thm:measurement} can be checked directly with data in which both instruments exist for the same people.

\paragraph{Data.} We use the 2017--2018 cycle of the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey \citep{nchs2020}: the 4{,}034 adults aged 20 or more with measured and self-reported height and weight, systolic blood pressure (mean of up to three readings), waist circumference, education and income-to-poverty ratio. We do not use the survey weights and treat the sample as the population. Self-reported BMI is computed from the reported weight and height and rounded to 0.1, the precision of the published measured BMI, for comparability. Self-reported BMI is on average 0.81 lower than measured BMI (standard deviation 1.99), and the gap grows with BMI: from 0.00 in the lowest quartile of measured BMI to $-1.66$ in the highest, in line with the literature on self-reported anthropometry \citep{connorgorber2007}. Using it in one channel only is differential measurement in the sense of Theorem~\ref{thm:measurement}; that the error is not classical (it grows with BMI) is what makes the bias sizeable.

\paragraph{Design.} Each replication assigns every person to the target group (a synthetic premium channel) with probability $\sigma\bigl(-1+\beta_b z(\mathrm{BMI})+0.5\,z(\mathrm{age})-0.4\,z(\mathrm{income})\bigr)$, where BMI is the measured value, $z$ standardizes and $\beta_b=0.8$; about 30\,\% end up in the target group. The channel is synthetic, but covariates, outcomes and measurement error are real. Since the assignment depends only on the true covariates, latent exchangeability and support hold by construction, and $\theta$, the mean outcome of the target group, is observed in each replication. We compare four specifications, all with age, sex, race, education and income: (A) measured BMI in both groups; (B) self-reported BMI in the target group and measured BMI in the donors, the channel-measurement case of Theorem~\ref{thm:measurement}; (C) without BMI; (D) self-reported BMI in both groups. The estimator is the doubly robust one with five-fold cross-fitting, with a penalized logistic classifier and a ridge regression, both additive in cubic splines; there are 200 replications. The replications share the population and only the assignment varies, so we evaluate bias, not coverage.

\paragraph{Registered predictions.} Before running, we computed the limit of specification B from Theorem~\ref{thm:measurement}(3), with $\mu$, fitted on the whole sample, an estimate of $m_0(v,b)=\E[Y\mid V=v,\mathrm{BMI}=b,D=0]$, which here equals $\E[Y\mid V=v,\mathrm{BMI}=b]$ because the assignment depends only on $(V,\mathrm{BMI})$: averaging over the assignment probabilities $a_i$, the predicted bias is $\sum_ia_i[\mu(V_i,b_i^{\mathrm{self}})-\mu(V_i,b_i)]/\sum_ia_i$, namely $-0.47$\,mmHg for blood pressure and $-2.44$\,cm for the waist. Strictly, Theorem~\ref{thm:measurement} assumes deterministic instruments, and the self-report is not a function of the measured value; but its proof goes through unchanged here, because the assignment depends only on $(V,\mathrm{BMI})$, so $\theta=\int m_0(V,\mathrm{BMI})\dd P^*$ while the estimator converges to $\int m_0(V,\mathrm{BMI}^{\mathrm{self}})\dd P^*$. The registered criterion was $|z|<3$, allowing a model error of up to 25\,\% of the predicted bias. We also predicted no bias in A, no mass of $\hat p$ close to 1 in A or B, an AUC difference between A and B below 0.02, and in C a negative bias larger in absolute value than that of B for the waist.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:nhanes} confirms the prediction of Theorem~\ref{thm:measurement} approximately: $-0.48$ against $-0.47$\,mmHg ($z=-0.3$) and $-2.56$ against $-2.44$\,cm. The waist deviation, 5\,\%, is within the registered tolerance but statistically distinguishable from zero ($z=-5.7$); net of the small bias of A, $-2.37$ against $-2.44$. Nothing in the observed data signals the problem. No target unit has $\hat p>0.99$, and $\neff/n_0$ \emph{improves} from 0.41 to 0.50: the target group, selected for high BMI, reports a BMI shifted down toward the donors, so the groups separate less and the weights look better precisely when the estimator is biased (noise alone, in D, moves $\neff/n_0$ only to 0.44). The AUC falls from 0.743 to 0.715, but specification A is not available in practice, so this fall cannot be observed. Dropping BMI is much worse ($-10.70$\,cm), because BMI drives the assignment. Two registered predictions fail: A has a bias of $-0.18$\,cm in the waist ($z=-8.3$), which grows with the strength of the assignment and vanishes with LightGBM ($-0.01$; Table~\ref{tab:nhanes-robust}), so it comes from the spline models; and the AUC falls by more than the registered 0.02.

Specification D, registered as exploratory, suggests a practical lesson with limits: using the self-reported BMI in \emph{both} groups halves the bias of the waist relative to B, but not that of blood pressure ($-0.47$ against $-0.48$). Measuring both groups with the same imprecise instrument leaves residual confounding, but does not evaluate the outcome model at a shifted argument; which effect dominates depends on the outcome. Table~\ref{tab:nhanes-robust} shows that for the waist D beat B in all four designs, by 24 to 73\,\%, whereas for blood pressure it was worse in two of them. We have no general result on when this ordering holds.

\paragraph{Robustness.} We varied the strength with which BMI drives the assignment and replaced the splines by LightGBM, again registering the predictions before running (Table~\ref{tab:nhanes-robust}). For each design we registered five: B within the tolerance above; the bias of A in the waist below 20\,\% of that of B (a relative criterion, chosen after the base run had shown a model bias in A; under $|z|<3$, A would also fail in the weak and strong designs); no target unit with $\hat p>0.99$ in A or B; C negative and larger than B in absolute value in the waist; and D smaller than B in absolute value in the waist. Of these 15 predictions, 14 hold. In all four designs, no target unit has $\hat p>0.99$ and $\neff/n_0$ is larger in B than in A. The failure is blood pressure with LightGBM: $-0.20$ against $-0.47$ ($z=4.0$). Blood pressure depends weakly on BMI and is noisy, so LightGBM flattens that dependence and transmits only part of the measurement error, while the prediction used $\mu$ fitted with splines. An unregistered check supports this: with $\mu$ fitted by LightGBM, the predicted bias is $-0.31$, and the observed $-0.20$ is at $z=1.7$ from it (for the waist, $-2.40$ instead of $-2.44$). When the outcome depends weakly on the mismeasured covariate, the prediction itself depends on the outcome model.
