\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The main lesson is that the most dangerous failure of reweighting with channel data is not a lack of overlap but a lack of comparability in how covariates are recorded. When a variable exists in only one channel, support breaks; the ideal classifier shows it, although estimated classifiers may show it only faintly. When the channels measure it differently, support can survive, the estimators target a wrong value that the data cannot distinguish from the truth, and the overlap diagnostics can look better than with correct measurement. Checking that both channels record each covariate in the same way is therefore a design question to settle before estimation, not something the diagnostics will reveal afterwards.

\paragraph{Practical recommendations.} The theory, the simulation and the public-data analyses suggest seven guidelines for evaluating a channel switch with observational data:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Exclude from the covariates the variables recorded by only one channel when they do not predict the basic-channel outcome given the others (Proposition~\ref{prop:drop}; this cannot be tested directly and must be argued), and diagnose lack of support through the mass of $\hat p$ or $\hat e$ close to 1, or through large estimated weights on the $\pi$-invariant scale (Proposition~\ref{prop:breaks}), complemented by univariate checks of range, ceilings and heaping by channel, which catch failures at the edge of a top-coded variable that classifiers miss (Section~\ref{sec:lalonde}).
  \item Check that each covariate is recorded in the same way in both channels. When differential measurement preserves support, no check on the data detects it (Theorem~\ref{thm:measurement}), and the overlap diagnostics may even improve (Section~\ref{sec:nhanes}); external information is needed. If one channel measures a covariate less accurately and the accurate version cannot be obtained, consider using the less accurate version in both channels as well, and compare: in our illustration it reduced the bias for the waist in every design but not consistently for blood pressure, and we have no general result.
  \item Use the doubly robust estimator with cross-fitting by unit, bearing in mind that without support it extrapolates the outcome model (Corollary~\ref{cor:drnosupport}).
  \item If the target group is a cutoff on a known score, estimate the weights with the channel propensity, using all units of the premium channel, and impose the cutoff (Proposition~\ref{prop:cutoff}). With good overlap this reduced the dispersion substantially in our simulation; under poor overlap it did not, so compare it with the standard classifier and, if trimming, trim within the doubly robust estimator (Section~\ref{sec:res-cutoff}).
  \item For the interval, use Wald with large samples. With medium samples and known-cutoff weights, both Wald and the unit-level Bayesian bootstrap were close to nominal in the simulation (Wald 91--92.5\,\% over 200 replications; the Bayesian bootstrap 90--95\,\% over only 40); with the standard classifier, the bootstrap is conservative. Be wary if $\neff/n_0$ is far below what is expected.
  \item Do not carry the sign of trimming from the true weights over to the estimated ones without checking it. If trimming, do it within the doubly robust estimator and compute the standard error with the untrimmed weights; in our simulation this was conservative with 30{,}000 customers but undercovered (78--88.5\,\%) with 3{,}000.
  \item Accompany the estimate with a sensitivity analysis, with $\Lambda$ calibrated against the observed covariates through the channel model (Lemma~\ref{lem:calibration}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitations.} The simulation evidence comes from a single data-generating process, built so that each result except Theorem~\ref{thm:measurement} had a scenario to test it, and the public-data illustrations are either semi-synthetic (the channel is assigned by us, and the predictions rest on a fitted superpopulation model whose class matters when the outcome depends weakly on the mismeasured covariate) or rest on a single experimental benchmark. Part 3 of Theorem~\ref{thm:measurement} requires the donor instrument to be injective; when it is coarse, or noisy with error that is non-differential with respect to $Y(0)$ in the donor channel, the weighting functional is still $\int m_0\dd P$ under $P\ll Q$, but $m_0(x)=\E_{Q^*}[\mu^*(X^*)\mid\varphi_0(X^*)=x]$ then depends on the latent distribution of the donors, as in regression calibration, and the shift has no closed form in terms of $\mu^*$ alone. Quantitative conclusions from the simulation, such as the gains of the known cutoff or the excess of the bootstrap, should be read as illustrations of the mechanisms, not as general magnitudes. The sensitivity bounds assume the MSM, which does not hold in the strict sense with unbounded confounders. The simulated coverage is evidence outside the strong-overlap condition of Theorem~\ref{thm:normality}. We treat the threshold that defines the target group as fixed. And the estimand is the counterfactual mean under the basic channel as it is today: if receiving the diverted units congests it, (A1) fails.

\paragraph{Open problems.} Five questions remain open: asymptotic normality of the known-cutoff doubly robust estimator, whose influence function we give in Section~\ref{sec:cutoff}; a theory explaining the excess of the bootstrap with noisy estimated weights and the validity of the Bayesian bootstrap with known-cutoff weights; the variance contribution of a threshold estimated as a quantile of the score, which relates to choosing the threshold itself; combining the sensitivity analysis with lack of support (Corollary~\ref{cor:bounds}; \citealp{manamkoong2025}) and extending it to condition (3) of Proposition~\ref{prop:drop} and to differential measurement; and a general result on when measuring both groups with the same imprecise instrument beats differential measurement (Section~\ref{sec:nhanes}).
